IB SEHS IA criteria and rubric explained

The SEHS IA (first assessed in 2026) is marked out of 24 using four equally weighted criteria. Each is worth 6 marks and 25% of the IA. Conclusion and Evaluation together carry half of the marks. This page explains the IB level descriptors in plain words. Your teacher uses the official wording.

How the marking works

  • Each criterion has four levels: 0, 1-2, 3-4 and 5-6.
  • Your teacher uses a best-fit approach. If your work matches different parts of a descriptor at different levels, the mark reflects the balance. Not every point of a descriptor has to be met.
  • Within a level, the upper mark is for work that largely meets the descriptor, and the lower mark is for work that meets it to a lesser extent.
  • Only whole numbers are recorded.
  • The criteria are independent. A high level in one does not guarantee a high level in another.
  • The top level does not mean faultless work. It is meant to be reachable.
  • Your teacher marks the work, and the IB externally moderates it.

Research design (6 marks)

This criterion is about how well you communicate your method: its purpose and the practice. It has three strands.

  • Research question and context. At the bottom, the question is stated with no context. In the middle, it is outlined within a broad context. At the top, it is described within a specific and appropriate context.
  • Methodological considerations. These move from stated, to described, to explained. They must be about collecting relevant and sufficient data to answer the question.
  • Reproducibility. At the bottom, there is not enough detail to repeat the investigation. In the middle, it can be repeated with few ambiguities or omissions. At the top, it can be repeated.

A question with context refers to the independent and dependent variables, or to two correlated variables, briefly describes the system the question sits in, and includes background theory that is directly relevant.

Methodological considerations include: how you measure the variables, the data source or model and how you sample it, the range, interval and frequency of the independent variable, repetitions and precision, control variables and how you control them, and any safety, ethical or environmental issues.

Data analysis (6 marks)

This criterion is about how you record, process and present the data in ways that suit the research question. It has three strands.

  • Communication. Recording and processing is communicated, then clear or precise, then clear and precise. Clear means the method of processing is easy to follow. Precise means following conventions: annotating graphs and tables correctly, and using units, decimal places and significant figures properly.
  • Uncertainties. Limited evidence at the bottom, some evidence with significant omissions or inaccuracies in the middle, and an appropriate consideration at the top.
  • Processing. Some processing with major omissions or inaccuracies at the bottom, some significant gaps in the middle, and processing that is appropriate and accurate at the top.

Your data is quantitative, or quantitative together with qualitative observations. Major omissions are the ones that stop you from drawing a valid conclusion. Significant ones leave you able to draw a conclusion, but with limits to its validity or detail.

Conclusion (6 marks)

This criterion is about how well you answer your research question, using your analysis and the accepted scientific context. It has three strands.

  • The conclusion itself. It moves from stated but not supported by the analysis, to described but not fully consistent with it, to justified and fully consistent with the analysis.
  • Scientific context. A superficial comparison at the bottom, some relevant comparison in the middle, and a relevant comparison that justifies the conclusion at the top.
  • Practical implications. Stated, then outlined, then explained.

A fully consistent conclusion interprets the processed data together with its uncertainties. Scientific context can come from published papers, published values, course notes, textbooks or other sources, and your citations must be detailed enough for the source to be traced. Practical implications are real-world applications related to health or performance.

Evaluation (6 marks)

This criterion is about how well you evaluate your methodology and suggest improvements. It has two strands.

  • Weaknesses and limitations. Generic at the bottom, specific in the middle, and at the top you explain the relative impact of specific weaknesses and limitations.
  • Improvements. Realistic improvements are stated, then described and linked to the weaknesses you identified, then explained.

Generic means a point that could apply to many methods and not to yours specifically. Weaknesses could relate to control of variables, precision of measurement or variation in the data. Limitations could be how far the conclusion is limited by the range of the data, the confines of the system, or the assumptions you made. Methodological means the whole approach as well as the procedural steps.

What to do with this

  1. Use each strand as a checklist for your own draft.
  2. Look at the words that change from level to level: stated, described, explained, justified. They show how much reasoning the marker expects.
  3. Make the evidence easy to find, with clear headings and numbered figures.
  4. Remember that your teacher gives your marks. IB IA Review compares your draft to these criteria and shows where evidence is missing.
