SEHS IA data analysis: tables, graphs, statistics and uncertainty

Data analysis is worth 6 marks. It rewards clear recording, suitable processing and an honest treatment of variation. You do not need advanced statistics, but you do need to explain what you did and why.

Communicate clearly and precisely

  • Clear means a reader can easily follow how you processed the data.
  • Precise means following conventions: correctly annotated graphs and tables, and the right units, decimal places and significant figures.

Record raw data well

  • Use one table per measure, with a clear title and units in the column headings.
  • Keep the same number of decimal places as your equipment can measure.
  • Keep trials and participants in order so that each value can be traced.
  • If you have a lot of data, show a sample in the report and explain where the rest is.

Process data to suit your question

  • Calculate means for repeated trials or groups.
  • Show variation with range or standard deviation.
  • Calculate change or percentage change when you compare before and after.
  • Show a sample calculation so that every processed value can be traced back to your raw data.
  • Use a spreadsheet to process data and draw graphs.

Choose graphs that fit

  • Bar charts with error bars for comparing groups.
  • Line graphs for changes over time.
  • Scatter plots with a trend line for relationships between two measures.
  • Label both axes with units and give each graph a title that states the finding.

Uncertainty and variation

Every measurement has some uncertainty. This is more than the smallest division of an instrument. It includes the reaction time of the timer, differences between participants and changes in effort from trial to trial. State it, and say whether it is small compared with the difference you found. If you add error bars, say what they show (standard deviation, standard error or a confidence interval). If the difference between your groups is within the variation, say so honestly and cautiously.

Statistical tests

A test such as a t-test can help you judge whether a difference is likely to be due to chance. The test must match your design: a paired test for the same participants measured twice, an unpaired test for separate groups. A correlation shows that two things are linked, not that one causes the other. Explain what a p-value means for your research question. A p-value with no explanation will not earn credit.

Common mistakes

  • Graphs without units or titles.
  • Averaging data that should be looked at participant by participant.
  • Using a test that does not match the design, such as an unpaired test on repeated measures.
  • Treating a correlation as proof of cause.
  • Hiding the raw data in an appendix when the analysis depends on it.
  • Describing a trend in the graph that the numbers do not support.
