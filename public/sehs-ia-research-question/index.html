How to write a good SEHS IA research question

A good research question makes the rest of your IA easier. It tells you what to measure, who to test and what your conclusion has to answer.

What a strong question contains

  • A clear measurement method for your variables, and a note of the context in which you test.
  • An independent variable you can change, such as a type of warm-up, a rest period or a hydration level.
  • A dependent variable you can measure, such as sprint time, jump height, heart rate or reaction time.
  • A clear group and setting, such as school-level swimmers or recreational runners.
  • A direction or comparison so the answer is measurable.

A simple template

How does [independent variable] affect [dependent variable] in [group] during [task]?

Example: How does a 3-minute dynamic warm-up compared with static stretching affect 20 m sprint time in school-level football players?

Weak versus stronger

  • Weak: Does warming up help? → Stronger: How does a 5-minute dynamic warm-up compared with no warm-up affect 10 m sprint time in school-level basketball players?
  • Weak: Does music improve exercise? → Stronger: How does listening to fast-tempo music affect distance covered in a 6-minute run in recreational runners?
  • Weak: Which diet or supplement is best for athletes? → This cannot be done in an SEHS IA. IB examiner instructions do not allow experiments that give participants any substance, such as supplements, caffeine or energy drinks. Choose a variable you can change without giving anything to the participants.

Checks before you commit

  1. Is it safe, and allowed under IB rules? You need your teacher's approval, and written consent from each participant.
  2. Can you measure the dependent variable accurately with the equipment you have?
  3. Can you collect enough data with the time and participants available?
  4. Can you control the other variables?
  5. Can someone else repeat your method from your description?

Add scientific context

In your report, explain in a short paragraph why you expect a certain result. Use ideas from the syllabus and from published research. This context is part of what the Research design criterion looks for.
